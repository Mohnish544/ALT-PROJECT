HYPERTENSION — CLINICAL MANAGEMENT GUIDE
Synthetic Educational Document for MedLens Demo
================================================
[Note: This document is entirely synthetic, created for demonstration purposes.
It is not a substitute for clinical guidelines from authoritative bodies.]

1. DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION
---------------------------------
Hypertension (high blood pressure) is defined as a sustained elevation of
arterial blood pressure (BP) at or above 130/80 mmHg according to the 2017
ACC/AHA Guidelines, or 140/90 mmHg according to the older JNC 7 classification.

Blood Pressure Classification (ACC/AHA 2017):
  Normal:             < 120/80 mmHg
  Elevated:           120–129 / < 80 mmHg
  Stage 1 HTN:        130–139 / 80–89 mmHg
  Stage 2 HTN:        ≥ 140 / ≥ 90 mmHg
  Hypertensive Crisis: > 180 / > 120 mmHg

Hypertension is classified as:
- Primary (Essential) Hypertension: No identifiable secondary cause (~95% of cases)
- Secondary Hypertension: Due to identifiable cause (renal artery stenosis,
  primary aldosteronism, pheochromocytoma, Cushing syndrome, etc.)

2. EPIDEMIOLOGY
---------------
Hypertension affects approximately 1.28 billion adults globally (WHO, 2021),
making it one of the leading preventable causes of cardiovascular morbidity
and mortality. Prevalence increases with age; more than 70% of adults over
65 years are affected. Hypertension is often asymptomatic ("the silent killer"),
leading to underdiagnosis and undertreatment.

3. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
------------------
Blood pressure is determined by cardiac output (CO) and systemic vascular
resistance (SVR): BP = CO × SVR.

Key pathophysiological mechanisms in essential hypertension include:
- Increased activation of the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS),
  leading to sodium and water retention and vasoconstriction.
- Increased sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity elevating heart rate
  and vasoconstriction.
- Endothelial dysfunction reducing nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability.
- Arterial stiffening (arteriosclerosis) reducing vascular compliance.
- Genetic polymorphisms in sodium transport and RAAS genes.
- Chronic kidney disease contributing to volume overload.

4. RISK FACTORS
---------------
Non-modifiable:
  - Age (risk increases significantly after 55 in men, 65 in women)
  - Family history of hypertension or premature cardiovascular disease
  - Race/ethnicity (higher prevalence in Black adults)
  - Sex (higher in men < 55 years; higher in women after menopause)

Modifiable:
  - High dietary sodium intake (> 2.3 g/day)
  - Obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²)
  - Physical inactivity
  - Excessive alcohol consumption
  - Tobacco use / smoking
  - Diabetes mellitus
  - Dyslipidemia
  - Chronic stress
  - Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)
  - Dietary patterns (low potassium, low calcium, high processed food)

5. CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND DIAGNOSIS
---------------------------------------
Most patients are asymptomatic. When symptomatic:
  - Headache (typically occipital, morning)
  - Dizziness or lightheadedness
  - Visual disturbances (in severe hypertension or hypertensive retinopathy)
  - Epistaxis (nosebleeds)

Hypertensive Urgency: Severe elevation (> 180/120 mmHg) WITHOUT target organ damage.
Hypertensive Emergency: Severe elevation WITH acute target organ damage
  (e.g., hypertensive encephalopathy, NSTEMI, aortic dissection, acute pulmonary edema).

Diagnostic Approach:
  1. Confirm diagnosis with two or more separate readings on two separate occasions.
  2. Use properly calibrated sphygmomanometer; patient seated for ≥ 5 minutes.
  3. Use ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) when white-coat or
     masked hypertension is suspected.
  4. Evaluate for secondary causes if onset is young (<30 years), resistant
     to treatment, or has atypical features.
  5. Assess for target organ damage: ECG (LVH), fundoscopy (retinopathy),
     urinalysis (proteinuria), serum creatinine (renal function).

6. TARGET ORGAN DAMAGE
-----------------------
Uncontrolled chronic hypertension leads to:

Cardiovascular:
  - Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) → heart failure
  - Accelerated coronary artery disease (CAD) → myocardial infarction
  - Aortic aneurysm / aortic dissection

Cerebrovascular:
  - Ischemic stroke (thrombotic or embolic)
  - Hemorrhagic stroke
  - Transient ischemic attack (TIA)
  - Vascular dementia

Renal:
  - Hypertensive nephrosclerosis → chronic kidney disease → end-stage renal disease
  - Microalbuminuria is an early marker

Ocular:
  - Hypertensive retinopathy (A/V nicking, flame hemorrhages, papilledema)
  - Retinal vein occlusion

7. NON-PHARMACOLOGICAL TREATMENT (LIFESTYLE MODIFICATION)
----------------------------------------------------------
Lifestyle modification is the cornerstone of hypertension management and is
recommended for all stages.

Dietary Approaches:
  - DASH Diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension):
    Rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy.
    Reduces BP by 8–14 mmHg systolic.
  - Sodium restriction: < 2.3 g/day (ideally < 1.5 g/day for high-risk patients).
    Reduces BP by 2–8 mmHg systolic.
  - Potassium intake: 3,500–5,000 mg/day from dietary sources (bananas, leafy greens).
  - Limit alcohol: ≤ 2 drinks/day for men, ≤ 1 drink/day for women.
    Reduces BP by 2–4 mmHg systolic.

Physical Activity:
  - Aerobic exercise: ≥ 150 minutes/week of moderate intensity
    (brisk walking, cycling, swimming).
    Reduces BP by 4–9 mmHg systolic.
  - Resistance training: 2–3 times per week as adjunct.

Weight Reduction:
  - Each 1 kg reduction in body weight reduces systolic BP by ~1 mmHg.
  - Target BMI < 25 kg/m² where achievable.

Smoking Cessation:
  - Smoking increases cardiovascular risk independently; cessation is essential.
  - Nicotine replacement therapy, varenicline, or bupropion may assist.

Stress Reduction:
  - Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) and cognitive behavioral therapy
    may modestly reduce BP.

8. PHARMACOLOGICAL TREATMENT
-----------------------------
Indication for medication:
  - Stage 1 HTN with high CVD risk or established CVD
  - Stage 2 HTN (BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg)
  - When lifestyle modification is insufficient after 3–6 months

First-Line Drug Classes:

  a) ACE Inhibitors (ACEi)
     Examples: Lisinopril, Enalapril, Ramipril
     Mechanism: Block conversion of angiotensin I → II; reduce RAAS activity.
     Indications: Preferred in diabetes (especially with proteinuria), CKD, heart failure.
     Side effects: Dry cough (10–15%), hyperkalemia, angioedema (rare, serious).
     Contraindications: Pregnancy, bilateral renal artery stenosis.

  b) Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs)
     Examples: Losartan, Valsartan, Olmesartan
     Mechanism: Block AT1 receptor directly; similar benefits to ACEi.
     Preferred over ACEi in patients with ACEi-induced cough.
     Side effects: Hyperkalemia, rare angioedema.

  c) Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
     Examples: Amlodipine (dihydropyridine); Diltiazem, Verapamil (non-DHP)
     Mechanism: Block L-type calcium channels in vascular smooth muscle.
     Indications: Elderly patients, isolated systolic hypertension, angina.
     Side effects: Peripheral edema (amlodipine), constipation (verapamil),
       bradycardia (non-DHP).

  d) Thiazide Diuretics
     Examples: Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ), Chlorthalidone
     Mechanism: Inhibit Na-Cl cotransporter in distal tubule → natriuresis.
     Indications: Effective monotherapy; good add-on agent.
     Side effects: Hypokalemia, hyperuricemia, hyponatremia, glucose intolerance.

Second-Line Drug Classes:
  - Beta-blockers (Metoprolol, Carvedilol) — preferred if heart failure or post-MI
  - Loop diuretics (Furosemide) — if volume overload or CKD
  - Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (Spironolactone) — resistant HTN
  - Alpha-blockers (Doxazosin) — if BPH coexists
  - Central agonists (Clonidine) — last resort due to rebound effects

Combination Therapy:
  - Most patients require ≥ 2 drugs to achieve target BP.
  - Preferred combinations:
    ACEi + CCB, ACEi + Thiazide, ARB + CCB, ARB + Thiazide
  - Avoid combining ACEi + ARB (increased renal risk, no added BP benefit).

Resistant Hypertension:
  Defined as BP above goal despite ≥ 3 antihypertensive drugs including a diuretic.
  - Confirm adherence and white-coat hypertension.
  - Add Spironolactone (25–50 mg) as 4th agent — most effective add-on.
  - Evaluate for secondary causes.

9. BLOOD PRESSURE TARGETS
--------------------------
General adults (ACC/AHA 2017):      < 130/80 mmHg
Elderly (≥ 65 years):               < 130/80 mmHg if tolerated
Diabetes mellitus:                  < 130/80 mmHg
Chronic kidney disease:             < 130/80 mmHg (or < 140/90 if proteinuria)
Heart failure:                      < 130/80 mmHg

10. MONITORING AND FOLLOW-UP
-----------------------------
- Initiate or adjust therapy, then recheck in 2–4 weeks.
- Once at goal, monitor every 3–6 months.
- Annual labs: electrolytes, renal function, glucose, lipid panel.
- Annual ECG for LVH surveillance.
- Annual fundoscopy (ophthalmology referral) in long-standing or severe HTN.
- Home blood pressure monitoring encouraged for patient engagement.

11. SPECIAL POPULATIONS
------------------------
Pregnancy:
  - Avoid ACEi and ARBs (teratogenic).
  - Preferred agents: Labetalol, Methyldopa, Nifedipine.
  - Severe gestational HTN or preeclampsia: IV Labetalol or IV Hydralazine.

Elderly:
  - Initiate at lower doses; titrate slowly to reduce fall risk.
  - Orthostatic hypotension is a concern.
  - Thiazides and CCBs generally well-tolerated.

Chronic Kidney Disease:
  - ACEi or ARB preferred (nephroprotective — reduces proteinuria).
  - Avoid NSAIDs, nephrotoxic agents.
  - If eGFR < 30, switch to loop diuretics from thiazides.

Diabetes Mellitus:
  - ACEi or ARB first line (reduce diabetic nephropathy progression).
  - Target BP < 130/80 mmHg.
  - Monitor HbA1c alongside BP for comprehensive cardiovascular risk management.

12. PROGNOSIS
-------------
Well-controlled hypertension significantly reduces:
  - Stroke risk by 35–40%
  - Myocardial infarction risk by 20–25%
  - Heart failure risk by > 50%

With combination of lifestyle modification and optimal pharmacotherapy,
most patients can achieve and maintain target blood pressure.

13. PATIENT EDUCATION POINTS
-----------------------------
- "White coat hypertension" — elevated BP only in clinical settings — should
  be confirmed with home monitoring before initiating treatment.
- Medications must be taken consistently even when feeling well.
- Sodium labeling: "low sodium" = < 140 mg/serving; read food labels carefully.
- Avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) as they raise BP and reduce antihypertensive efficacy.
- Report side effects promptly (especially ACEi cough or ankle swelling with CCBs).
